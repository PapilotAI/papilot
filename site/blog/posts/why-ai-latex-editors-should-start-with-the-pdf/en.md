It begins with a hesitation on page seven. You are reading a proof in your own manuscript, following the spine of an argument you have lived with for months, when a subscript in a display equation catches your eye. The notation is technically legal, yet it quietly blurs the boundary between observed samples and held-out data. Two paragraphs below, an inline symbol inherits the ambiguity; across the gutter, a figure caption leans on the same shorthand. The thought arrives whole, in the visual language of the page. You know immediately what the reader will stumble over, and you know what you want the manuscript to say instead.

Then you reach for your tools, and the page disappears.

To act on an observation made in the rendered PDF, conventional scientific writing environments ask you to leave the manuscript behind and descend into raw `.tex` source. For decades, that split between the page where scholars think and the markup where changes are executed was accepted as the necessary tax of LaTeX. Even now, as capable AI agents enter research workflows, most tools have left that old geometry untouched. They place a smarter assistant inside the code pane, keep raw markup at the center of the screen, and treat the typeset paper as a secondary preview at the end of a pipeline.

We created [Papilot](https://github.com/PapilotAI/papilot) from a different premise: when humans and AI collaborate on LaTeX, the rendered PDF should become the shared interface of conversation. The typeset page is where intention forms, where revisions take on meaning, and where scholarly judgment finally rests. Papilot gives the primary workspace to the manuscript itself and reserves no permanent pane for a source editor. Underneath, your project remains an ordinary local folder of `.tex` and `.bib` files—always openable in whatever external text editor you prefer—but the daily dialogue between author and AI moves onto the page.

## Why Source-Centered AI Collaboration Misses the Manuscript

The persistence of the split screen is not an accident of implementation; it is an inherited assumption about where writing happens. In Overleaf’s [redesigned editor](https://docs.overleaf.com/getting-started/how-do-i-use-overleaf/redesigned-overleaf-editor), the editing pane sits on the left while the compiled PDF viewer occupies the right; authors move between Code and Visual modes, resize or collapse panes, recompile to refresh the output, and jump back and forth with SyncTeX. Even when code is hidden or softened by a visual mode, the surface where you edit remains structurally separate from the typeset page your reader will actually see.

Adding an AI assistant to the source side of that divide does not heal the split. As documented in OpenAI’s [Prism troubleshooting guide](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/20001050-troubleshooting-prism), an in-editor assistant can generate LaTeX snippets with Keep or Undo controls, surface inline errors, edit project files, and trigger recompilation alongside a PDF preview pane. Yet the interaction remains anchored to markup. The human author is still asked to translate a visual, structural intention on the manuscript into a source-level conversation, and then translate a stream of proposed `.tex` edits back into a mental picture of the page.

That translation is where momentum goes to die. Suppose you ask an agent to untangle that ambiguous estimator on page seven. In a source-centered workspace, the response arrives as red-and-green diffs across macros, `\mathcal` wrappers, nested subscripts, and alignment tokens. Before you can judge the mathematics, you are forced to act as a human compiler—simulating spacing, line breaks, and macro expansions in your head—only to recompile and hunt through a preview pane to verify whether the page now reads as you intended. Source diffs tell you what happened to the commands. They do not show you what happened to the paper.

## When the Page Becomes the Shared Context

Everything shifts when the manuscript stops acting as a passive output and becomes the place where conversation starts and ends. When you and your AI agent look at the same rendered page, pointing replaces searching.

In Papilot, a revision begins with the gesture a coauthor would make across a desk. You draw a box around the problematic equation on page seven, highlight the sentence where the notation drifts, and attach a concise note explaining how the sample indices should be separated. You do not hunt through included files for the right `\begin{equation}` block, and you do not paste raw markup into a chat sidebar. The rendered PDF supplies the spatial and rhetorical context directly.

Once you dispatch those notes, your connected AI agent works where code agents belong: inside the underlying local LaTeX files. It locates the definitions, updates the equation and surrounding prose, and hands the modified `.tex` source to the compiler. When compilation finishes, you are not greeted by a wall of markup hunks. Instead, Papilot presents the rendered before-and-after changes right on the manuscript page. You see the old equation and the new one in their actual typographic proportions, beside the figure and prose they have to live with, and you choose whether to accept the revision, reject it, or leave a follow-up comment for another pass.

![Papilot interface showing a rendered manuscript page with a boxed equation and a highlighted sentence, each attached to an author note queued for the AI agent.](/assets/blog/notes.en.png "Annotations sit directly on the typeset equation and surrounding prose, turning the PDF into a shared surface for expressing revision intent.")

Treating the PDF as the interface does not turn your paper into a locked binary or a guessing game over source-less pages. Papilot only runs on real local LaTeX projects. Plain text files remain the ground truth on disk, and Git remains the record of your project’s history. Review decisions inside Papilot are deliberately distinct from version control: accepting a rendered change records your editorial verdict, while applying changes to your working tree, committing, and pushing stay explicit steps in your own hands. Whenever you want to tweak a custom preamble or inspect raw `.tex` directly, your favorite external editor is a window away.

## Clarity That Sharpens Judgment

There is a deeper stake in moving the interface from source to manuscript, and it concerns authorship itself. As AI agents grow fluent at reshaping proofs, harmonizing notation across sections, and compressing hesitant prose, a harder danger comes into view: plausible error beneath flawless typesetting. An agent can produce a clean, typeset page that subtly weakens a hypothesis, drops a boundary condition, or overstates what a curve in Figure 3 actually demonstrates.

If an author spends their attention decoding LaTeX diffs and navigating split panes, less attention remains for the harder question of truth. By clearing away markup mediation and letting you inspect changes before and after directly on the typeset page, Papilot is designed to concentrate scholarly scrutiny, not relax it. Visual clarity does not validate an argument; only the author can decide whether a lemma still holds and whether every sentence can be defended. When the mechanics of `.tex` recede into the background, responsibility comes into sharper focus.

For a long time, writing with machines meant learning to see our own work through the machine’s intermediate representations. We learned to think in source trees, token diffs, and split views, treating the finished page as a distant reward at the end of compilation.

Now return to page seven. The subscript that blurred your assumption is boxed on the margin; the revised equation sits cleanly in the line; the prose below it says exactly what the proof requires. You have not left the argument to manage the markup. The manuscript is once again a single place—where you read, where you converse with AI, and where you stand behind every word on the page.
